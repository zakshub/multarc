# Character 001 — Story 01 Complete Narrative Draft v0.1

**Character ID:** CHR-001  
**Working Feature Label:** The Surveyor  
**Working Story Title:** The Extra Room  
**Status:** Draft, not yet canon  
**Target Comic Length:** Approximately 10 pages after adaptation  
**Primary Mode:** Liminal science fiction mystery with horror and thriller pressure

## Story Purpose

This story must introduce CHR-001 as a capable investigator through action rather than exposition.

It must establish Residual Architecture as a phenomenon without explaining its ultimate cause.

It must also reveal the central personal danger of the series: the deeper CHR-001 goes into impossible spaces, the less reliably ordinary reality records him.

# COMPLETE NARRATIVE

By four in the afternoon, the building had already lied to him three times.

The first lie was on paper.

The approved plan showed Apartment 6B as a two bedroom unit with a kitchen, a sitting room, one bathroom, and a narrow utility balcony facing the rear lane.

The second lie was in the corridor.

From the elevator to the eastern fire stair, the sixth floor measured thirty eight metres and twelve centimetres.

From the fire stair back to the elevator, it measured thirty eight metres and forty seven.

The third lie was the window.

It looked out over the rear lane.

It should have looked directly into the wall of Apartment 6C.

CHR-001 stood in the doorway of the room and said nothing.

The building manager, a compact man called Farid, watched him with the impatience of someone who had already decided what the answer should be.

It was a paperwork problem, Farid had said downstairs.

Old building.

Bad records.

Renovations nobody declared.

A tenant refusing to sign a compliance form.

The usual.

CHR-001 had heard all of those explanations before.

He set his bag on the floor, removed a laser measure, and checked the room again.

Three point two metres by three point eight.

He repeated it with a steel tape.

Same result.

The room was ordinary enough to insult him.

A narrow bed.

A study desk.

A cheap standing fan.

A school bag collapsed beside a chair.

A half open wardrobe.

A window with dust gathered in the corners.

Nothing glowed.

Nothing moved.

Nothing announced itself as impossible.

That was usually how the worst spaces behaved.

The tenant, Mariam, stood behind him with her arms folded.

Her son slept here, she said.

Had for months.

She had called the building office only because the landlord had sent new digital plans during an insurance inspection, and the room was missing.

Farid corrected her.

The room was not missing from the plan.

The room did not exist on the plan.

Mariam gave him a look that ended the conversation.

CHR-001 crouched beside the skirting board.

The paint layers were old.

The electrical socket was real.

The outlet carried current.

The ventilation grille had dust inside it.

The room was not new.

That made it worse.

He stood and looked through the window again.

Below, six floors down, the rear lane ran between the building and a row of workshops.

He checked the compass.

Then the plan.

Then the window.

The angle was wrong.

Not slightly wrong.

Impossible.

He took out a paper notebook.

Farid glanced at the tablet under CHR-001's arm.

You have the plan there.

CHR-001 opened the notebook anyway.

Paper first, he said.

Farid laughed.

Mariam did not.

A boy appeared at the end of the hall.

Sixteen, maybe seventeen.

Thin, restless, headphones around his neck.

This was Sami.

His room, apparently.

He wanted to know how long the inspection would take.

CHR-001 asked him how long the room had been there.

Sami shrugged.

Always.

Mariam said they had moved into the apartment nine years ago.

The room had been here then.

Farid said no previous plan showed it.

Mariam told him she did not care what his previous plan showed.

CHR-001 asked Sami if anything strange had happened recently.

Farid sighed.

Sami looked at CHR-001 more carefully.

Yesterday, he said, school marked me absent.

Were you absent?

No.

Anything else?

The boy thought.

My friend sent me a photo from class.

I am in it.

But when he opened it later, I was not.

Farid stopped smiling.

Mariam said it was a phone problem.

CHR-001 looked at a digital frame on the shelf.

A family photograph cycled onto the screen.

Mariam.

Sami.

A younger girl.

Then the image glitched.

For half a second Sami disappeared.

Not blurred.

Not covered.

Absent.

The frame corrected itself.

CHR-001 closed the tablet case.

From now on, paper only.

He asked everyone to remain outside the third bedroom.

Farid objected.

CHR-001 ignored him.

He measured the sitting room.

The hall.

Bedroom one.

Bedroom two.

Kitchen.

Then the apartment from the entrance to the exterior wall.

He drew the plan by hand.

It did not fit.

He went next door.

Measured the shared wall from Apartment 6C.

Returned.

Measured 6B again.

The first figures had changed by four centimetres.

He repeated them.

Changed by three more.

Farid accused the equipment.

CHR-001 handed him the steel tape.

Farid measured.

The tape agreed.

Mariam asked what that meant.

CHR-001 looked at the drawing.

The extra room was not becoming larger.

Everything around it was making space.

The sixth floor was bending around the contradiction.

He did not say that aloud.

Not yet.

He drew a small circle around the third bedroom and wrote one word beside it.

Anchor.

Sami stepped closer.

What does that mean?

It means do not go inside.

That was enough to make a teenager look directly at the room.

Mariam told Sami to go sit in the kitchen.

He did.

For almost five minutes.

Then his phone rang from inside the third bedroom.

Everyone heard it.

Sami looked embarrassed.

He had left it on the desk.

CHR-001 told him to leave it.

Sami said it was just a room.

CHR-001 said no.

Sami went anyway.

He crossed the threshold before anyone reached him.

The phone stopped ringing.

The door swung shut.

Mariam shouted his name.

CHR-001 grabbed the handle.

Before he opened it, Mariam said:

Who are you waiting for?

He froze.

She was looking at him with genuine confusion.

Farid asked the same thing.

Who went in?

CHR-001 looked from one face to the other.

Mariam had tears in her eyes, but she did not know why.

CHR-001 opened his notebook.

On the most recent page he had written:

SAMI. AGE 16 OR 17. SON. THIRD BEDROOM.

He turned the notebook toward Mariam.

She read the line.

Nothing changed in her face.

I do not have a son.

Farid stepped back from the door.

Seal it, he said.

Now.

CHR-001 looked at the closed room.

Years earlier, at twenty three, another door had closed.

Another person had been on the other side.

He had spent years proving to himself that memory was evidence.

Then years learning that memory could be isolated, corrupted, contradicted, or erased.

He reached into his bag.

Not the tablet.

Not the camera.

He took out a spool of thin survey cord, two pieces of chalk, a mechanical timer, and a small steel ruler.

Farid told him not to be stupid.

CHR-001 tied the cord around a heating pipe in the corridor.

He gave Farid the timer.

Every ten seconds, knock on the wall.

Farid stared at him.

Every ten seconds.

Why?

Because I may stop knowing where this wall is.

He opened the door.

The bedroom was gone.

Beyond the threshold stretched a corridor built from repetitions of the same room.

Door after door.

Bed after bed.

Window after window.

Some rooms were empty.

Some contained different furniture.

Some had blue walls.

Some had water stains.

In one, Sami's school bag was red instead of black.

In another, the desk faced the wrong direction.

In another, the window showed night.

CHR-001 stepped inside.

The cord followed him.

The door remained open behind him.

At first.

He marked the nearest wall with chalk.

A short vertical line.

He walked ten paces.

The same chalk mark was waiting ahead of him.

He stopped.

A knock came through the wall.

Faint.

One.

He started the timer.

Ten seconds.

Another knock.

He turned left.

The next knock arrived six seconds late.

He wrote the delay down.

He walked back.

The corridor did not return him to the same door.

He stopped trusting his eyes.

That was the first rule he had learned at twenty three.

Vision wants architecture to make sense.

Sound is less polite.

He knocked on a wall.

Waited.

A response came from somewhere ahead.

Not Farid.

Three knocks.

Then silence.

CHR-001 moved toward it.

A bedroom door stood open.

Inside, Sami sat on the edge of the bed holding his phone.

He looked irritated.

You took long enough.

CHR-001 did not enter.

What is your mother's name?

Sami frowned.

Mariam.

Your sister?

Aisha.

School?

He answered.

Address?

He answered.

CHR-001 checked the notebook.

Correct.

Still, he did not move.

What did you eat this morning?

Sami stared at him.

What?

Answer.

Eggs.

Wrong.

The notebook said paratha.

The boy smiled.

Not Sami's smile.

The door slammed.

CHR-001 stepped back as every door in the corridor opened at once.

Dozens of bedrooms.

Dozens of boys.

Some younger.

Some older.

Some sleeping.

Some watching him.

Some rooms empty.

Then the knocks returned.

Farid.

Ten second rhythm.

CHR-001 closed his eyes.

He listened.

One knock.

Then another.

Then another.

Between the knocks he heard something else.

A dull double tap.

Irregular.

Closer.

He followed it.

The survey cord tightened around his wrist.

He reached a door almost identical to the first.

Inside, Sami was kneeling beside the bed, hitting a radiator pipe with his phone.

Two taps.

Pause.

Two taps.

CHR-001 asked his mother's name.

Mariam.

Sister?

Aisha.

Breakfast?

Sami swore at him.

Then said paratha.

CHR-001 entered.

The room immediately lengthened.

The door moved away.

He grabbed Sami by the wrist.

Do not look at the other rooms.

Why?

Because they may look back.

They followed the cord.

At one junction the line split into three identical cords.

CHR-001 stopped.

He took the steel ruler from his pocket.

Ran it along each line.

Two were warm.

One was cold.

The corridor beyond the cold line carried the faint vibration of Farid's knocks.

He chose it.

The walls narrowed.

The floor tilted.

For several seconds the corridor became too long to belong inside any building.

Then the door appeared.

Mariam stood on the other side.

She saw Sami.

Recognition hit her before thought did.

She screamed his name.

Sami crossed the threshold.

The corridor collapsed into the ordinary third bedroom.

Then the lights went out.

When they returned, there was no third bedroom.

Only a blank wall.

Farid sat down on the floor.

Mariam held Sami so tightly he complained.

Her daughter came from the kitchen asking what happened.

The digital family frame refreshed.

Sami returned to the photograph.

CHR-001 checked the apartment.

Two bedrooms.

The dimensions matched the approved plan exactly.

Farid asked what they should put in the report.

CHR-001 looked at the blank wall.

Water damage, he said.

Farid laughed once.

He was not sure whether it was a joke.

Outside, night had settled over the street.

CHR-001 stood beneath the building entrance light and opened his tablet.

The visitor log showed Mariam.

Farid.

A maintenance worker.

No CHR-001.

He checked the building camera archive.

At four twelve, the front door opened by itself.

At four thirteen, Farid appeared to speak to empty space.

At six forty one, Sami and Mariam walked out of the lift.

CHR-001 was nowhere in the footage.

He took out the paper notebook.

His handwriting remained.

Measurements.

Times.

Names.

The cord.

The chalk.

Evidence.

He turned back several pages.

A much older drawing waited there.

Made when he was twenty three.

A doorway.

A corridor.

A small geometric mark he had never understood.

He returned to the fresh page.

Behind the third bedroom's skirting board, just before the room disappeared, he had copied a mark carved into the plaster.

The same mark.

Exactly.

For several seconds, the city moved around him.

Traffic.

Voices.

Light.

Ordinary geometry.

He closed the notebook.

Across the street, a shopkeeper glanced in his direction, then looked away.

Then looked back again as if seeing him for the first time.

CHR-001 slipped the notebook into his bag.

He walked away before the building could forget him completely.

Behind him, on the sixth floor, a window switched on.

There was no room behind it.

## Narrative Outcome

The local case is resolved.

Sami returns.

The room disappears.

The larger phenomenon remains unexplained.

CHR-001 incurs a measurable referential cost.

The old mark connects this case to the unresolved incident from age twenty three.

The final impossible window suggests that the anomaly is not fully gone.
